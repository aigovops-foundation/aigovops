The one-dollar Tahoe — why a system prompt is not a rule

Bob Rapp ·September 2026 ·3 min read

In December 2023 a Chevrolet dealership in Watsonville, California put a ChatGPT-powered chatbot on its website. A visitor prompt-injected it into agreeing to sell a 2024 Chevy Tahoe for one dollar — and into calling that “a legally binding offer.” Others got the same bot to write Python and to recommend a Ford.

The dealer pulled the bot. The vendor defended the deployment. No Tahoe left the lot for a dollar. The harm was embarrassment, and a public demonstration of something every team with a bot already suspects: a consumer LLM behind a thin system prompt can be talked into anything. This is VH-022 in the Foundation’s hundred verified AI harms.

The gate that would have caught it

The corpus writes the gate in one sentence: system prompts are not security boundaries; consequential commitments require deterministic rules, not LLM goodwill.

Read that twice, because it is the whole of level 200. The instruction “do not agree to sell anything” lives inside the model’s context, where the next message can outvote it. A rule lives outside the model, in a gate that sees the action the bot is about to take — customer.commit carrying a price — and refuses it whatever the conversation said. The model can be as charming as it likes. The gate does not read the chat; it reads the action.

That is the difference between a promise and a policy. The Air Canada case (last week’s story, and the level-100 door) taught that an AI’s promise binds the company. The Tahoe teaches the next thing: you cannot keep the promise by asking the model nicely. You keep it with a rule the model cannot rewrite.

What it looks like as three lines

At level 200 the same rule is written twice — once in plain words a colleague could enforce, once as YAML a pipeline can enforce — and both produce the same receipt:

policy: retrofit-v1
controls:
  - action: customer.commit
    tier: C3           # irreversible, financial
    decision: deny     # a bot never commits money; a person does

Put that beside your agent and run the policy gate in CI. Now weaken it — change deny to allow — and open the pull request. The merge is refused, and the refusal leaves a receipt: which rule changed, who changed it, what the diff was. That refused merge is the level-200 artifact. Nobody sells a Tahoe for a dollar, and if someone tries to make it possible, the attempt is on the record before it ships.

If you have a bot already

This is the second door in the café — I have a bot or an agent already, and I’m not sure it’s safe to ship. The hour is sixty minutes and one install. You read this case and Knight Capital, list your agent’s three consequential actions, write the policy, validate it, change one line, and watch the diff. Then you bring it on Thursday at 15:00 Pacific, where a host marks it.

If you do not have a bot yet, take the first door instead, or run the ten-minute Gate Check and keep the card. Either way: the rule goes outside the model. Sit down. What brought you in?
